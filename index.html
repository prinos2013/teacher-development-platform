1. Methods of Teaching Overview

Teaching methods generally fall along a spectrum between Teacher-Centered and Student-Centered approaches. The most effective educators fluidly move between them based on the learning objective, the complexity of the material, and the needs of their students.

2. Direct Instruction (Teacher-Centered)

Direct instruction is highly structured and led by the teacher. It is most effective for introducing foundational concepts and explicitly teaching new step-by-step procedures.

Introduction to Child-Centred Teaching

Child-centred teaching is an approach to education in which the learner is placed at the centre of the teaching and learning process. Instead of the teacher doing most of the talking and learners simply listening and memorising information, learners are actively involved in discovering, discussing, practising, and applying knowledge.

The Teacher's Shift: In child-centred teaching, the teacher does not become unnecessary. Rather, the teacher changes from being mainly a source of information to being a facilitator, guide, organiser, motivator, and assessor.

Core Emphases:

  • Active participation
  • Learning by doing
  • Learner experiences
  • Individual differences
  • Cooperation and interaction
  • Problem-solving
  • Critical thinking
  • Independent learning

Discussion & Questioning Methods Communication

Discussion Method: Learners and the teacher exchange ideas about a particular topic or problem. The teacher provides direction while learners express views, ask questions, and respond to others.

  • Advantages: Develops communication, builds confidence, encourages critical thinking.
  • Teacher's Role: Establish rules, ask guiding questions, correct serious misconceptions, and summarise.

Question-and-Answer: Used to introduce lessons, check prior knowledge, stimulate thinking, and maintain attention. Avoid poorly designed questions that only encourage memorisation.

Discovery & Inquiry-Based Learning Investigation

Discovery Learning: Allows learners to discover concepts or relationships through exploration rather than receiving information directly. (e.g., examining wood, stone, plastic, and metal to classify properties).

Inquiry-Based Learning: Involves learners investigating questions or situations to develop understanding.

Question → Investigation → Evidence → Analysis → Conclusion

Problem-Solving & Projects: Presenting real-life problems (e.g., water conservation at school) and guiding learners to identify, gather information, suggest solutions, and evaluate results over time.

Cooperative & Collaborative Learning Teamwork

Cooperative Learning: Learners work together in small groups to achieve a shared learning objective, featuring a common goal, individual responsibility, and positive interaction.

Collaborative Learning: Places greater emphasis on learners negotiating ideas and jointly constructing knowledge rather than just fulfilling structured roles.

Peer Teaching: Occurs when learners help teach or explain concepts to other learners under the teacher's supervision.

Activity & Experiential Learning Practical Skills

Activity-Based Learning: Acquiring skills through purposeful activities like experiments, drawing, sorting, and model-making.

Experiential Learning Cycle: Experience → Reflection → Understanding → Application.

Role-Play & Simulation: Acting out roles or creating artificial realistic situations (e.g., mock elections) to safely practise decision-making and empathy.

Field Trips: Organised educational visits outside the classroom to observe real objects, places, or activities (e.g., farms, markets, water treatment facilities).

Ghanaian Classroom Application

Child-centred approaches connect strongly with Constructivist theory (learners actively construct meaning based on previous knowledge and environmental interactions).

These approaches make learning highly practical in Ghana by substituting expensive resources with local materials:

  • Stones for counting and classification.
  • Empty containers for measurement activities.
  • Local plants in science lessons.
  • Community problems as the basis for problem-solving.
Example Lesson: Water Pollution (Integrated Science)
  1. Intro: Show pictures of clean/polluted water.
  2. Observe: Learners identify differences.
  3. Discuss: Groups identify local pollution causes.
  4. Investigate: Groups discuss possible effects.
  5. Present: Groups present findings to class.
  6. Solve: Suggest practical reduction methods.

Roles, Advantages & Challenges

Advantages:
  • Promotes active learning and improves understanding.
  • Develops critical thinking, creativity, and communication.
  • Increases motivation and improves retention.
  • Connects school knowledge to real-life communities.
Challenges:
  • Large class sizes make group activities difficult.
  • Limited resources, materials, or time.
  • Requires more pedagogical competence and preparation.
  • Active learning creates noise/movement management needs.
  • High-stakes exams often push teachers back to memorisation.

How Children Learn & Remember

Key Teaching Law: Children are not small adults. Their brains cannot process multiple ideas at the same time, and they forget very quickly if we do not connect lessons to things they can see, touch, or already know.

A child's memory system operates in three simple steps:

  • 1. The Spotlight (Sensory Attention): If the class is noisy or the blackboard is cluttered, the child's spotlight lands on the wrong thing.
  • 2. The Desk (Working Memory): This desk is tiny! It can only hold 2 to 4 items at once. If you give 5 instructions, the desk collapses and the child gets confused.
  • 3. The Storage Room (Long-Term Memory): Once a child practices an idea repeatedly with meaning, it gets stored here permanently.

Cognitive Load Theory (The "Water Cup" Model)

A child's working memory is like a plastic cup. It can only hold so much water before it overflows. When it overflows, learning stops entirely.
